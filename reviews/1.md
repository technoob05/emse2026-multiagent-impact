Summary
This paper studies whether public traces on GitHub actually evidence multi-agent collaboration when a pull request (PR) authored by one AI-coding product receives review activity from a different product. Using a pinned snapshot of the AIDev-pop dataset and strict event anchoring (exact reply-to identifiers and review-batch IDs), the authors separate co-presence from connected replies, next visible ownership, and later PR state. They find that cross-product “handoffs” are rare; most subsequent activity is by user accounts, and the only robust signal associated with higher subsequent merge probability is an exact, anchored reply to the trigger—written by a human in four out of five cases.

Strengths
Technical novelty and innovation
Introduces a clear “evidence ladder” separating co-presence, exact addressed edges, ownership of next visible action, and later PR outcome, preventing over-interpretation of mere co-occurrence.
Uses strict, machine-checkable anchoring via GitHub’s parent comment IDs and different review-batch IDs to rule out same-call artifacts and within-batch self-replies.
Proposes and executes a within-PR event-order shuffle baseline to challenge claims based on observed reviewer order—an important methodological addition beyond alphabetical or naive baselines.
Employs a time-varying exposure model (edge “switches on” when it occurs), complementing the 48-hour checkpoint design and strengthening temporal validity.
Experimental rigor and validation
Careful cohort construction: burst-window exclusions, matched pairs (cross- vs same-product) within repository/author/product/channel/month, and a clear 48-hour checkpoint to maintain temporal ordering.
Multiple robustness checks: repository clustering, leave-one-repo/pair-out, repository fixed effects, E-values and prevalence–impact grids, within-repo label shuffles (both for edge-outcome and issue-link analyses).
Transparent reporting of denominators and cohort sizes (e.g., 8,608 triggers, 546 matched pairs, 1,067 PRs for outcome cohort), with explicit acknowledgement of selection to “slower” PRs for RQ3.
Clarity of presentation
Very clear setup of constructs (trigger, addressed edge, relay/owner) and what each rule includes or excludes; effective use of schematic figures to illustrate filtering and evidence levels.
Distinguishes descriptive links from causal claims, repeatedly reminding readers of observational limits.
Thoughtful reporting of failed tests and limitations (e.g., small counts for mapped-product-to-mapped-product replies; between-repo vs within-repo differences for replier familiarity).
Significance of contributions
Provides a much-needed corrective: public multi-agent “co-presence” should not be equated with cross-agent collaboration; exact anchored replies from humans are the reliable public signal of acknowledgement.
Offers reusable baselines and measurement templates (burst filtering, within-PR event shuffles, edge anchoring) for future empirical work on LLM/agent workflows.
The issue-link result (only helpful across product boundaries) gives actionable insight about context-setting for agent-to-human workflows.
Weaknesses
Technical limitations or concerns
The “exact addressed edge” is limited to inline threads with reliable parent IDs; PR-level and some file-level reviews that lack precise anchors are largely outside scope, potentially undercounting responses and collaboration patterns at coarser granularities.
Reliance on AIDev identity mapping to six products leaves “other bots” and unlabelled automations as a grey area; some user accounts may script automation under user identities, risking misclassification of “user” as “human.”
The 48-hour outcome cohort excludes the majority of cross-product inline-trigger PRs (fast-closing/merging), limiting external validity of RQ3 to slower-moving PRs.
Experimental gaps or methodological issues
Burst-window thresholds (0/1/5/10/30 minutes) are plausible but somewhat arbitrary; product- or repo-specific timing distributions could motivate adaptive thresholds or data-driven cutoffs.
The matched-pairs analysis, while well-controlled, is relatively small (546 pairs) compared to the total triggers; additional power or complementary matching (e.g., entropy balancing) might strengthen cross- vs same-product contrasts.
Although the paper uses an E-value and placebo outcomes, stronger negative controls (e.g., pseudo-edges on unrelated comments or random “fake” anchors) could further stress-test the edge–outcome link.
Clarity or presentation issues
Some references and dates (e.g., “Monperrus 2026”) appear forward-dated; while likely due to dataset timeline, clarifying publication status would help.
A more explicit breakdown of where edges can and cannot be measured (inline vs file/PR level) up front, with quantitative coverage shares, would help readers gauge scope limitations earlier.
Missing related work or comparisons
Given that action-based reviewers and coarser-grained review comments are common in practice, a brief comparison to methods that reconstruct addressing at file/PR level (e.g., using diff-reconstruction or LLM-assisted adjudication) would contextualize the narrow but precise “exact edge” choice.
Discussion could better situate findings against studies showing CRA comment “signal” or actionable rates and human response rates, highlighting complementarities (addressed/anchored vs. actionable/semantic).
Detailed Comments
Technical soundness evaluation
The evidence ladder and strict anchoring rules are technically sound and well-motivated, mitigating common pitfalls (batch self-replies, burst-induced illusions of dialogue).
The within-PR shuffle baseline for reviewer order is a particularly strong design choice; it convincingly shows that product persistence patterns can be reproduced without invoking coordination.
The time-varying model that “activates” the edge exposure when it occurs further assuages concerns that the 48-hour restriction biases results to slow PRs.
The E-value (2.27) indicates moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding; combined with placebo and shuffle tests, the support for the main claims is credible for an observational study.
Experimental evaluation assessment
Cohort definitions are precise and reproducible (pinned dataset revision, identity registry, and event ID joins); reporting of denominators and leave-one-out checks increases trustworthiness.
The matched-pair framework for cross- vs same-product triggers is an appropriate design to probe boundary effects; the 13.4 pp deficit in follow-up for cross-product triggers looks consistent and is practically meaningful.
The edge–merge association (+17.3 pp adjusted) is probed with multiple alternative specifications (edge window variants, repository fixed effects, conservative controls) and remains positive, which is compelling even if not causal.
The issue-link difference-in-differences is carefully constrained to triggers that can receive an edge and to within-repository/month comparisons; the shuffled-label test bolsters the interpretation.
Comparison with related work (using the summaries provided)
Prior studies have shown that CRA-only reviews merge less often than human-only reviews and that AI-generated review comments often have low actionable “signal.” This paper’s precise anchoring complements those works by focusing on a verifiable connection (exact reply) rather than semantic actionability; both views cohere around the conclusion that human engagement is decisive.
Compared to works cataloging AI review actions and reconstructing addressing at coarser granularity, this paper trades recall for precision. The approach avoids false positives inherent in coarse anchoring but may miss broader classes of addressed feedback—this trade-off is acknowledged and appropriate for the paper’s measurement goal.
The reviewer-order shuffle baseline adds a methodological contribution beyond comparisons against alphabetical or simplistic baselines reported elsewhere.
Discussion of broader impact and significance
The study cautions against using “product counts” as a proxy for collaboration and argues for reporting maps of observable connections and ownership instead—a message with immediate methodological implications for the burgeoning literature on agent workflows.
The findings suggest practical guidance: to foster cross-boundary engagement, provide richer task context (issue links), and track anchored acknowledgements as a progress signal in field deployments.
The work refrains from causal claims; nonetheless, its measurement templates and baselines are likely to influence best practices for evaluating multi-agent claims in open-source ecosystems.
Questions for Authors
How large is the coverage gap between inline comments (with reliable reply anchors) and other review modalities (file- or PR-level) in your cohorts? Could you quantify what fraction of total review interactions these anchorable events represent?
To what extent might “user accounts” include scripted automation or organization-run services that are not flagged as bots? Did you attempt heuristics (e.g., temporal burst patterns, repeated exact templates) to separate human-operated from automated user accounts?
How sensitive are the RQ1 ownership and relay results to the chosen burst thresholds across different products or repositories? Have you considered a data-driven threshold (e.g., change-point detection on inter-event times) or product-specific windows?
For the matched-pair analysis (546 pairs), could you share the distribution across product pairs and repositories? Does the -13.4 pp follow-up gap persist for the most common pairs individually?
In RQ3, the edge–merge association concentrates among repliers with prior review history in the repository but disappears within-repo. Can you provide more detail on repository-level moderators (e.g., governance style, contribution volume) that might explain this?
Did you explore negative-control exposures (e.g., pseudo-edges assigned to unrelated comments) to bolster the specificity of the addressed-edge mechanism beyond the placebo outcomes?
For RQ4, issue links help across product boundaries but not within the same product. Do you have hypotheses—perhaps from qualitative inspection—about whether this is due to human cognitive load, perceived legitimacy, or triage cues?

(Calibrated to ICLR scale)
This is a careful, well-executed empirical study that advances the measurement of collaboration in agentic software workflows. Its central contribution—disentangling co-presence from verifiable, anchored replies and from later outcomes—sharply challenges a common but weak proxy (counting products). The methodological additions (exact reply anchoring, burst filtering, within-PR event shuffles, time-varying exposure) are thoughtful and reusable, and the results are consistently supported across multiple robustness checks. The main limitations are scope (focus on inline, anchorable replies) and residual confounding typical of observational analyses, along with external validity constraints for the 48-hour outcome cohort. Nevertheless, the paper’s insights are timely and significant for the ICLR community working on LLM agents, evaluation, and human–AI workflows. I recommend acceptance, with encouragement to expand the discussion of coverage limits and to explore extensions to coarser-grained review modalities in future work.
